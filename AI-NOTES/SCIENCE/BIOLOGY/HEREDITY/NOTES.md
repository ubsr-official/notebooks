# Heredity - Class 10 Biology Notes (CBSE)

## Quick Definitions List

**Heredity** - Transmission of traits from parents to offspring through genes

**Variation** - Differences between individuals of the same species

**Gene** - Section of DNA that codes for a specific protein; unit of heredity

**Allele** - Alternative forms of a gene (e.g., T and t for height)

**Dominant trait** - Trait expressed even when only one copy of allele is present (represented by capital letter)

**Recessive trait** - Trait expressed only when both copies of allele are present (represented by lowercase letter)

**Genotype** - Genetic makeup of an organism (e.g., TT, Tt, tt)

**Phenotype** - Observable characteristics/physical expression of genes (e.g., tall, short)

**Homozygous** - Both alleles identical (TT or tt)

**Heterozygous** - Two different alleles (Tt)

**F1 generation** - First filial generation; offspring from cross of two parents

**F2 generation** - Second filial generation; offspring from cross of F1 individuals

**Monohybrid cross** - Cross involving one trait/character

**Dihybrid cross** - Cross involving two traits simultaneously

**Chromosome** - Thread-like structure carrying genes in linear sequence

**Homologous chromosomes** - Paired chromosomes (one maternal, one paternal)

**Sex chromosomes** - Chromosomes determining sex (X and Y in humans)

**Autosomes** - Non-sex chromosomes (22 pairs in humans)

**Gamete** - Reproductive cell with haploid number of chromosomes

**Diploid (2n)** - Cell with two sets of chromosomes (body cells)

**Haploid (n)** - Cell with one set of chromosomes (gametes)

**Meiosis** - Cell division producing four haploid gametes from one diploid cell

## 1. Accumulation of Variation During Reproduction

### Why Variation Matters

- **Variation = survival tool** for species
- Provides raw material for evolution and adaptation
- Enables some individuals to survive environmental changes
- Different individuals have different advantages in different conditions

### Sources of Variation

#### In Asexual Reproduction:
- **Very limited variation**
- Only source: DNA copying errors (mutations)
- Example: Sugarcane fields show minimal variation
- Like making photocopies - nearly identical with minor errors

#### In Sexual Reproduction:
- **Maximum variation**
- Two sources:
  1. DNA copying errors (mutations)
  2. **Genetic recombination** (mixing of parental genes)
- Example: Human siblings show distinct differences despite same parents
- Like shuffling two card decks together - creates entirely new combinations

### Selection of Variations

- **Not all variations are equal**
- Environment acts as filter (natural selection)
- Advantageous variations → better survival → more offspring
- Example: Heat-resistant bacteria survive fever better
- Variations drive evolution and species adaptation

## 2. Mendel's Laws of Heredity

### Why Mendel Chose Pea Plants (Critical Details)

**7 Key Reasons Pea Plants Were Perfect:**

1. **Contrasting traits** - Clear either/or characteristics (no intermediates)
   - Tall vs Short (no medium height)
   - Round vs Wrinkled seeds
   - Yellow vs Green seeds
   - Purple vs White flowers
   - Inflated vs Constricted pods
   - Green vs Yellow pods
   - Axial vs Terminal flowers

2. **Self-pollinating** - Naturally self-fertilize, preventing contamination
   - Petals seal tightly, preventing entry of foreign pollen
   - Enables creation of **pure-breeding lines** (true-breeding strains)

3. **Easy cross-pollination** - Can manually transfer pollen between plants
   - Remove male parts before maturity
   - Transfer pollen from desired plant
   - Complete experimental control

4. **Short generation time** - Quick results (one growing season)
   - Study multiple generations rapidly

5. **Large offspring numbers** - Many seeds per plant
   - Statistically significant data
   - Accurate ratio calculations

6. **Easy cultivation** - Simple to grow in garden/monastery
   - Low maintenance requirements

7. **Bisexual flowers** - Contain both male and female reproductive organs
   - Enables both self and cross-pollination

### Mendel's Revolutionary Approach

**What Made Mendel Different:**
- **First to use mathematics in biology** - counted and calculated ratios
- Studied traits over multiple generations systematically
- Used large sample sizes (hundreds/thousands of plants)
- Analyzed data quantitatively, not just qualitatively
- Proposed particulate inheritance (factors/genes), not blending

## 3. Monohybrid Cross (One Trait Inheritance)

### Mendel's Classic Experiment: Tall × Short Pea Plants

**Pure-breeding parents:**
- **P generation:** TT (tall) × tt (short)

**F1 Generation Result:**
- **All offspring:** Tt (ALL TALL)
- **Key observation:** No medium height plants
- **Conclusion:** Tallness is dominant, shortness is recessive

**F2 Generation (F1 × F1 self-pollination):**
- **Phenotypic ratio:** 3 Tall : 1 Short (3:1)
- **Genotypic ratio:** 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (1:2:1)

### Punnett Square for Monohybrid Cross

```
     T    t
   +----+----+
T  | TT | Tt |  → Tall
   +----+----+
t  | Tt | tt |  → Tall / Short
   +----+----+
```

**Result:** 3/4 tall (75%), 1/4 short (25%)

### Law of Dominance (Mendel's First Law)

**Statement:** In a heterozygote, one allele (dominant) can mask the expression of another allele (recessive)

**Key Points:**
- Dominant allele needs only ONE copy to express (T)
- Recessive allele needs BOTH copies to express (tt)
- Dominant ≠ better/stronger; just means "expressed when present"
- Recessive ≠ rare/weak; can be common in populations

**Molecular Basis:**
- Dominant allele (T): produces functional enzyme → gibberellin hormone → tall plant
- Recessive allele (t): produces defective/no enzyme → little gibberellin → short plant
- In Tt: one functional copy produces enough hormone for tall phenotype

## 4. Dihybrid Cross (Two Traits Inheritance)

### Mendel's Two-Trait Experiment

**Pure-breeding parents:**
- **P generation:** RRYY (round, yellow) × rryy (wrinkled, green)

**F1 Generation:**
- **All offspring:** RrYy (ALL round and yellow)
- Both dominant traits expressed

**F2 Generation (F1 × F1 self-pollination):**

**Phenotypic Ratio: 9:3:3:1**
- 9/16 Round Yellow (both dominant)
- 3/16 Round Green (1st dominant, 2nd recessive)
- 3/16 Wrinkled Yellow (1st recessive, 2nd dominant)
- 1/16 Wrinkled Green (both recessive)

### Punnett Square for Dihybrid Cross

```
Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry (from each parent)

        RY      Ry      rY      ry
    +-------+-------+-------+-------+
RY  | RRYY  | RRYy  | RrYY  | RrYy  |
    +-------+-------+-------+-------+
Ry  | RRYy  | RRyy  | RrYy  | Rryy  |
    +-------+-------+-------+-------+
rY  | RrYY  | RrYy  | rrYY  | rrYy  |
    +-------+-------+-------+-------+
ry  | RrYy  | Rryy  | rrYy  | rryy  |
    +-------+-------+-------+-------+

9 = Round Yellow | 3 = Round Green
3 = Wrinkled Yellow | 1 = Wrinkled Green
```

### Law of Independent Assortment (Mendel's Second Law)

**Statement:** Alleles of different genes assort independently during gamete formation

**Key Points:**
- Genes for different traits separate independently
- Creates NEW trait combinations not seen in parents
- Example: Round-Green and Wrinkled-Yellow (new combinations in F2)
- **Only applies to genes on different chromosomes**

**Why It Happens:**
- Genes located on different chromosomes
- During meiosis, chromosome pairs separate randomly
- Maternal/paternal chromosome 1 assorts independently of chromosome 2
- Creates genetic diversity through recombination

**Important Note:** Genes on the SAME chromosome are linked and do NOT assort independently (exception to the law)

## 5. How Genes Control Traits

### The Central Dogma: DNA → RNA → Protein → Trait

**Step-by-Step Mechanism:**

1. **DNA contains the code**
   - Gene = specific section of DNA
   - Codes for one specific protein
   - Written in 4-letter alphabet (A, T, G, C)

2. **Gene makes protein**
   - DNA transcribed to mRNA in nucleus
   - mRNA translated to protein by ribosomes
   - Protein = chain of amino acids

3. **Protein performs function**
   - Enzymes catalyze reactions
   - Structural proteins build tissues
   - Hormones regulate processes
   - Proteins determine traits

4. **Function produces trait**
   - Example: Height in peas
     - T gene → functional enzyme → gibberellin hormone → cell elongation → TALL
     - t gene → defective enzyme → little gibberellin → limited elongation → SHORT

### Why Two Gene Copies?

**Organisms are diploid (2n) - every gene exists in two copies:**

**Advantages:**
- **Backup protection:** If one copy damaged, other compensates
- **Variation storage:** Recessive alleles hidden but preserved
- **Equal parental contribution:** Fairness in sexual reproduction
- **Regulatory flexibility:** Complex control of gene expression

## 6. Gamete Formation and Meiosis

### The Chromosome Problem

**The Mathematical Issue:**
- Body cells: 2n (diploid) - humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs)
- If gametes were diploid: offspring would have 4n (92 chromosomes!)
- Each generation would double chromosome number → impossible

**The Solution: Meiosis**
- Special cell division ONLY for gamete production
- Reduces chromosome number by half
- Diploid (2n) → Haploid (n)
- Humans: 46 → 23 chromosomes in gametes

### How Meiosis Works

**Two Divisions, Four Products:**

**Meiosis I (Reduction Division):**
- Homologous chromosome pairs separate
- Chromosome number reduced: 2n → n
- **Random assortment occurs here** (independent assortment)
- Maternal and paternal chromosomes distribute randomly

**Meiosis II (Similar to Mitosis):**
- Sister chromatids separate
- No further reduction in chromosome number
- Four haploid cells produced

**Result:**
- 4 genetically unique haploid gametes
- Each gamete has one chromosome from each homologous pair
- Random combination creates diversity

### Fertilization Restores Diploid Number

**Process:**
- Haploid sperm (n) + Haploid egg (n) = Diploid zygote (2n)
- Example in humans: 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes
- Maintains species chromosome number across generations

## 7. Sex Determination in Humans

### Chromosomal Sex Determination

**Human Chromosome Composition:**
- **Total:** 46 chromosomes = 23 pairs
- **22 pairs:** Autosomes (same in males and females)
- **1 pair:** Sex chromosomes (different in males and females)

**Sex Chromosome Patterns:**
- **Females:** XX (homogametic - produce one type of gamete)
- **Males:** XY (heterogametic - produce two types of gametes)

### The Y Chromosome and SRY Gene

**Critical Detail:**
- Y chromosome carries **SRY gene** (Sex-determining Region of Y)
- SRY gene = master switch for male development
- SRY protein triggers:
  - Testes formation
  - Male hormone production
  - Male reproductive system development

**Default Pathway:**
- Without Y chromosome (no SRY gene) → default female development
- XX → ovaries → female characteristics

### Mechanism of Sex Inheritance

**Parental Contributions:**

**Mother (XX):**
- Can only produce X-bearing eggs
- All eggs contain X chromosome

**Father (XY):**
- Produces two types of sperm:
  - 50% carry X chromosome
  - 50% carry Y chromosome

### Sex Determination Punnett Square

```
        X(egg)  X(egg)
       +-------+-------+
X(sperm)| XX   | XX    |  → Female
       +-------+-------+
Y(sperm)| XY   | XY    |  → Male
       +-------+-------+
```

**Result:** 1:1 ratio (50% XX female, 50% XY male)

**Key Point:** Father's sperm determines child's sex, NOT mother

### Common Misconceptions Clarified

**MYTH 1:** "Mother determines baby's sex"
- **FACT:** Father's sperm (X or Y) determines sex
- Mother always contributes X

**MYTH 2:** "After 3 boys, next child more likely to be girl"
- **FACT:** Each pregnancy is independent event
- Always 50:50 probability regardless of previous children
- This is the Gambler's Fallacy

**MYTH 3:** "Can control baby's sex naturally through diet/timing"
- **FACT:** Sex determination is random in natural conception
- No scientifically proven method to influence sex naturally

## 8. Key Genetic Concepts

### Genotype vs Phenotype

| Aspect | Genotype | Phenotype |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| Definition | Genetic makeup | Observable trait |
| Visibility | Cannot see | Can see/measure |
| Example | TT, Tt, tt | Tall, Short |
| Representation | Letters | Words/descriptions |

**Critical Understanding:**
- Different genotypes can produce same phenotype
  - TT → Tall
  - Tt → Tall (also tall because T is dominant)
  - tt → Short
- Cannot always determine genotype from phenotype alone

### Homozygous vs Heterozygous

**Homozygous (Pure):**
- Both alleles identical
- Two types:
  - **Homozygous dominant:** TT (produces all T gametes)
  - **Homozygous recessive:** tt (produces all t gametes)
- Breeds true (all offspring identical when self-pollinated)

**Heterozygous (Hybrid):**
- Two different alleles: Tt
- Produces two types of gametes: 50% T, 50% t
- Shows dominant phenotype (tall)
- Carries hidden recessive allele

## 9. Important Clarifications from NCERT

### Why Equal Parental Contribution?

**Question from text:** How is equal genetic contribution of male and female parents ensured?

**Answer:**
- Both parents contribute exactly ONE set of chromosomes (haploid)
- Sperm (n) + Egg (n) = Zygote (2n)
- Each gamete formed through meiosis
- No parent contributes more genetic material than the other

### Understanding the 3:1 Ratio

**From NCERT Fig 8.3:**
- When Tt × Tt cross occurs
- **Genotypic ratio:** 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (1:2:1)
- **Phenotypic ratio:** 3 Tall : 1 Short (3:1)
- 3/4 = 75% show dominant trait
- 1/4 = 25% show recessive trait

### Test Cross Verification

**From Activity 8.2:** How to confirm F2 has 1:2:1 ratio?

**Answer: Test Cross**
- Cross F2 plants with homozygous recessive (tt)
- If F2 plant is TT: all offspring tall
- If F2 plant is Tt: 50% tall, 50% short
- If F2 plant is tt: all offspring short
- This reveals hidden genotype

## 10. Worked Examples for Exam Practice

### Example 1: Blood Group Problem (NCERT Question)

**Question:** Man with blood group A marries woman with blood group O. Daughter has blood group O. Is this enough to determine which trait is dominant?

**Answer:** 
- **NO**, this information is insufficient
- Reason: Father could be:
  - AA (homozygous) - but then daughter cannot be O
  - AO (heterozygous) - daughter can inherit O from both parents
- For daughter to have O, father must be AO
- This means A can be dominant over O
- But we cannot conclude with certainty from just one example
- Need more data from multiple families

### Example 2: Tall Plant with Violet Flowers (NCERT Exercise 1)

**Question:** Tall plant with violet flowers (TTWW or TtWw?) × Short plant with white flowers (ttww). All F1 have violet flowers, but half are short. What's the tall parent's genotype?

**Options:** (a) TTWW (b) TTww (c) TtWW (d) TtWw

**Solution:**
- All F1 violet → W is dominant, tall parent must have W
- Half F1 short → tall parent must be Tt (heterozygous)
- If parent was TT, all F1 would be tall
- **Answer: (c) TtWW**

**Verification:**
- TtWW × ttww
- F1: 50% TtWw (tall violet), 50% ttWw (short violet) ✓

### Example 3: Dominant Coat Color in Dogs (NCERT Exercise 3)

**Project Outline:**

**Step 1:** Select two pure-breeding dog lines
- One with black coat (assume BB)
- One with brown coat (assume bb)

**Step 2:** Cross them → F1 generation
- If all F1 are black: black is dominant
- If all F1 are brown: brown is dominant
- If all F1 intermediate: incomplete dominance

**Step 3:** Breed F1 × F1 → F2 generation
- Should get 3:1 ratio confirming dominance
- 3 dominant color : 1 recessive color

### Example 4: Light-Colored Eyes (NCERT Exercise 2)

**Question:** Children with light eyes likely have parents with light eyes. Can we determine if light eye color is dominant or recessive?

**Answer:**
- **NO, cannot determine from this information alone**
- **Explanation:**
  - If light is recessive (ll): light-eyed parents (ll × ll) → all light-eyed children ✓
  - If light is dominant (L): Ll × Ll → 75% light, 25% dark children
  - Study only says "likely," meaning some exceptions possible
  - Both scenarios could explain observation
- **Need:** Crosses between light × dark to determine dominance pattern

## 11. Common Misconceptions (Critical for Exams)

### Misconception 1: Recessive = Rare

**WRONG:** Recessive traits are rare in populations
**CORRECT:** Recessive traits can be very common
- Example: Type O blood is recessive but most common globally
- Dominance describes expression, NOT frequency

### Misconception 2: Acquired Traits are Inherited (Lamarckism)

**WRONG:** If I build muscles, my children inherit strong muscles
**CORRECT:** Only changes in DNA (germline mutations) are inherited
- Bodybuilder's muscles ≠ inherited by children
- Somatic (body cell) changes ≠ inherited
- Only germline (reproductive cell) changes inherited

**Note:** Epigenetics shows some environmental effects can influence gene expression patterns in limited, heritable ways, but this is NOT Lamarckian inheritance

### Misconception 3: Dominant = Better

**WRONG:** Dominant alleles are superior/beneficial
**CORRECT:** Dominant/recessive is about expression, not value
- Many genetic diseases caused by dominant alleles
- Many normal, healthy traits are recessive

### Misconception 4: Genes Alone Determine Traits

**WRONG:** Traits are 100% genetic
**CORRECT:** Most traits = genes + environment interaction
- Example: Height = genes + nutrition + health
- Genes provide potential range
- Environment determines where in range individual falls

## 12. Key Formulas and Ratios

### Monohybrid Cross Ratios

**Aa × Aa:**
- Genotypic: 1:2:1 (AA:Aa:aa)
- Phenotypic: 3:1 (dominant:recessive)

**Aa × aa (Test cross):**
- Genotypic: 1:1 (Aa:aa)
- Phenotypic: 1:1 (dominant:recessive)

### Dihybrid Cross Ratios

**AaBb × AaBb:**
- Phenotypic: 9:3:3:1
  - 9 = both dominant
  - 3 = first dominant, second recessive
  - 3 = first recessive, second dominant
  - 1 = both recessive

**AaBb × aabb (Test cross):**
- Phenotypic: 1:1:1:1 (all four combinations equally likely)

### Probability Rules

**Independent events:** Multiply probabilities
- Example: Probability of 3 girls = 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8

**Either/or events:** Add probabilities
- Example: Probability of boy OR girl = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1

## Summary: Core Principles

1. **Variation** is essential for evolution; sexual reproduction maximizes it
2. **Mendel's Laws** explain inheritance patterns through particulate factors (genes)
3. **Dominant alleles** mask recessive alleles in heterozygotes
4. **Independent assortment** creates new trait combinations (genes on different chromosomes)
5. **Genes → Proteins → Traits** (central dogma of molecular biology)
6. **Meiosis** creates haploid gametes; fertilization restores diploid number
7. **Sex determination** in humans is chromosomal (XX female, XY male)
8. **Genotype** determines potential; **phenotype** is observable outcome
