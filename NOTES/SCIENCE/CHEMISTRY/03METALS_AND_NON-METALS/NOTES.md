# Metals and Non-metals - Class 10 Chemistry Notes (CBSE)

## Quick Definitions List

**Metal** - Element that loses electrons to form positive ions; good conductor, lustrous, malleable, ductile

**Non-metal** - Element that gains electrons to form negative ions; poor conductor, dull, brittle

**Metallic lustre** - Shiny appearance of metal surface due to reflection of light by free electrons

**Malleability** - Property of metal to be beaten into thin sheets without breaking

**Ductility** - Property of metal to be drawn into thin wires without breaking

**Conductivity** - Ability to allow heat or electricity to pass through; metals are good conductors

**Sonorous** - Property of producing ringing sound when struck; characteristic of metals

**Brittleness** - Property of breaking easily when force applied; characteristic of non-metals

**Reactivity series** - Arrangement of metals in order of decreasing reactivity; determines displacement reactions

**Activity series** - Same as reactivity series; K most reactive, Au least reactive

**Displacement reaction** - More reactive metal displaces less reactive metal from its salt solution

**Amphoteric oxide** - Metal oxide that reacts with both acids and bases; Al₂O₃, ZnO

**Ionic compound** - Compound formed by transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal; electrovalent

**Electrovalent compound** - Same as ionic compound; held by electrostatic forces

**Cation** - Positively charged ion; formed when metal loses electrons

**Anion** - Negatively charged ion; formed when non-metal gains electrons

**Octet rule** - Atoms tend to gain/lose/share electrons to achieve 8 electrons in valence shell

**Mineral** - Naturally occurring element or compound in earth's crust; may or may not be useful

**Ore** - Mineral from which metal can be extracted profitably; commercially useful mineral

**Gangue** - Unwanted impurities (soil, sand, rocks) present in ore; must be removed

**Metallurgy** - Science of extracting metals from ores and refining them; includes several steps

**Enrichment** - Process of removing gangue from ore before extraction; concentration of ore

**Roasting** - Heating sulfide ore strongly in excess air to convert to oxide; produces SO₂

**Calcination** - Heating carbonate ore strongly in limited air to convert to oxide; produces CO₂

**Reduction** - Process of removing oxygen from metal oxide to obtain pure metal

**Thermit reaction** - Highly exothermic displacement reaction; Al + Fe₂O₃ → Fe + Al₂O₃; used for welding

**Electrolytic reduction** - Using electricity to reduce metal compounds; for highly reactive metals

**Electrolytic refining** - Purification of impure metal using electrolysis; impure metal as anode

**Anode mud** - Insoluble impurities that settle at bottom of anode during electrolytic refining

**Corrosion** - Slow degradation of metal surface due to reaction with environment (air, moisture)

**Rusting** - Corrosion of iron forming hydrated iron(III) oxide; requires both air and water

**Galvanization** - Coating iron/steel with zinc layer to prevent rusting; zinc corrodes preferentially

**Anodizing** - Forming thick protective oxide layer on aluminum by electrolysis

**Alloy** - Homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or metal and non-metal

**Amalgam** - Alloy where one component is mercury; liquid or semi-solid

**Aqua regia** - Mixture of conc. HCl and conc. HNO₃ (3:1); dissolves gold and platinum

---

```diff
! FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLE: Electron Configuration Determines Properties
! 
! Metals: Few electrons in outermost shell (1, 2, or 3)
!         → Easy to lose electrons → Form positive ions
!         → Electropositive character
! 
! Non-metals: Many electrons in outermost shell (5, 6, or 7)
!             → Tendency to gain electrons → Form negative ions
!             → Electronegative character
! 
! Noble gases: Complete outer shell (8 electrons, except He with 2)
!              → No tendency to gain/lose → Chemically inert
! 
! All chemical behavior flows from electron configuration!
```

## 1. Physical Properties of Metals - The Deep Understanding

### 1.1 Metallic Lustre - Why Metals Shine

**NCERT tells you:** Metals have shining surface called metallic lustre

**What NCERT doesn't explain - The electron sea model:**

```diff
! Metals have FREE ELECTRONS that aren't bound to specific atoms
! These electrons are delocalized (move throughout metal)
! This creates "electron sea" or "electron cloud"
! 
! When light hits metal surface:
! - Free electrons absorb photons
! - Electrons get excited to higher energy
! - Immediately fall back to ground state
! - Re-emit photons (light)
! - This happens in ALL directions
! 
! Result: Light is REFLECTED efficiently
! We see this as SHINE or LUSTRE
```

**Why metals lose lustre in air:**

```
Metal surface + Air → Thin oxide/sulfide layer forms

Example: Silver + H₂S (in air) → Ag₂S (black coating)
         Copper + CO₂ + H₂O → Basic copper carbonate (green)

Oxide/sulfide layer:
- Not metallic in nature
- Doesn't have free electrons
- Doesn't reflect light well
- Appears dull

Cleaning with sandpaper:
- Removes oxide/sulfide layer
- Exposes fresh metal underneath
- Lustre restored
```

**Why gold doesn't tarnish:**

```diff
! Gold is EXTREMELY unreactive
! Doesn't form oxide even at high temperature
! Doesn't react with sulfur in air
! No coating forms
! Permanent lustre
! This is why ancient gold ornaments still shine!
```

### 1.2 Malleability and Ductility - The Atomic Explanation

**Malleability:** Ability to be beaten into thin sheets

**Ductility:** Ability to be drawn into thin wires

**What NCERT doesn't explain - Why this happens:**

**Atomic structure of metals:**

```
Metals have LAYERED structure:

Layer 1: ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫
Layer 2:  ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫  
Layer 3: ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫ ⚫

Atoms arranged in regular pattern
Layers can SLIDE over each other
Free electrons act as "glue" holding layers together
```

**When force is applied:**

```
Malleability (hammering):

Before:
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫

Hammer hits ↓

After:
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫
(Spread into sheet)

Layers slide but DON'T break
Metal deformed but intact
```

**Why metals don't break:**

```diff
! Metallic bonding is NON-DIRECTIONAL
! Electrons shared among ALL atoms (electron sea)
! Not between specific pairs like covalent bonds
! 
! When layers slide:
! - Atoms in new positions
! - But still surrounded by electron sea
! - Bonding maintained
! - Structure intact
! 
! This is why metals are malleable and ductile!
```

**Comparison with ionic/covalent:**

```
Ionic compounds (like NaCl):
⊕⊖⊕⊖⊕⊖
⊖⊕⊖⊕⊖⊕
⊕⊖⊕⊖⊕⊖

If layers slide:
⊕⊕⊖⊖⊕⊕  ← Like charges come together
⊖⊖⊕⊕⊖⊖  ← Repulsion!

Result: Crystal BREAKS (brittle)
This is why NaCl shatters when hit

Metals vs Ionic:
Metals: Malleable (layers slide)
Ionic: Brittle (layers break)
```

```
Gold - Most malleable:

Gold - Most ductile:
```

### 1.3 Conductivity - Electrical and Thermal

**Why metals conduct electricity:**

```diff
! FREE ELECTRONS are the key
! 
! In metals:
! - Valence electrons not bound to atoms
! - Form delocalized electron cloud
! - Can move freely throughout metal
! - This movement = Electric current
! 
! When voltage applied:
! - Electrons drift toward positive terminal
! - Continuous flow of charge
! - This IS electricity
```

**The mechanism:**

```
Metal wire connected to battery:

⊝ Battery ⊕
  ↓      ↑
  e⁻ flow this way →

Inside wire:
Electrons move from atom to atom
Not individual electrons traveling end to end
Rather like domino effect - push propagates

Speed of current: ~Speed of light
Speed of individual electrons: Very slow (mm/s)
But EFFECT is instantaneous
```

**Why non-metals don't conduct:**

```
Non-metals (like sulfur, carbon - except graphite):

All electrons TIGHTLY bound to atoms
In covalent bonds between specific atoms
No free electrons available
Cannot move when voltage applied

Result: No current flow = Insulator
```

**Exceptions:**

```diff
! Graphite conducts electricity (only non-metal that does)
! 
! Why?
! Graphite structure:
! - Layers of carbon atoms
! - Each C bonds to 3 others
! - 4th electron FREE (delocalized)
! - Can move along layers
! - Hence conducts electricity
! 
! This is why graphite used in:
! - Electrodes
! - Pencil "lead" (leaves mark by conducting electrons)
```

---

**Heat conductivity:**

```
Why metals conduct heat:

Method 1: Free electron movement
- Electrons at hot end gain energy
- Move to cold end
- Transfer energy
- Very efficient heat transfer

Method 2: Atomic vibrations
- Hot atoms vibrate more
- Collide with neighbors
- Transfer kinetic energy
- Propagates through metal

Metals use BOTH methods
Non-metals use only Method 2 (slower)

Result: Metals = Good heat conductors
        Non-metals = Poor heat conductors (insulators)
```

**Applications:**

```
Cooking utensils:
- Made of Cu, Al (excellent conductors)
- Heat spreads evenly
- Food cooks uniformly

Handles made of:
- Wood or plastic (insulators)
- Don't get hot
- Safe to touch

Electric wires:
- Core: Cu or Al (conductors)
- Coating: PVC/rubber (insulators)
- Current flows in core
- Coating prevents shock
```

### 1.4 Sonority - The Ringing Property

**What is sonority:**

```
Metals produce ringing sound when struck
Non-metals produce dull thud

Why this happens:

When metal struck:
1. Atoms displaced from equilibrium
2. Elastic restoring forces pull back
3. Atoms vibrate in regular pattern
4. Vibrations persist (low damping)
5. Vibrations transfer to air
6. We hear as ringing sound

Non-metals:
- Irregular structure
- Vibrations damped quickly
- Dull sound
```

**Applications:**

```
School bells: Made of brass/bronze
- Loud, clear ring
- Audible from distance
- Malleability allows shaping

Cymbals, gongs: Brass/bronze
- Sustained vibration
- Musical instruments

Tuning forks: Steel
- Specific frequency
- Pure tone
- Standard for tuning
```

## 2. Chemical Properties of Metals - Reactivity Patterns

### 2.1 Reaction with Oxygen - Understanding the Pattern

**General reaction:**

```
Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide

Examples:
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (vigorous, white flame)
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃ (bright light, lot of heat)
2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO (slow, black coating)
```

**Why different metals react differently:**

```diff
! Reactivity depends on:
! 1. Ease of losing electrons (ionization energy)
! 2. Strength of metal-oxygen bond formed
! 
! Highly reactive metals (K, Na):
! - Very easy to lose electrons
! - Form very stable oxides
! - React violently
! - Catch fire in air
! - Stored in kerosene
! 
! Moderately reactive (Mg, Al, Zn):
! - Moderate ease of electron loss
! - React when heated
! - Form oxide layer
! 
! Less reactive (Cu, Ag):
! - Difficult to lose electrons
! - React very slowly
! - Need high temperature
! 
! Unreactive (Au):
! - Very difficult to lose electrons
! - Don't form oxide even when heated
! - Permanent shine
```

---

**The protection mechanism - Oxide layer:**

```
Aluminum paradox:
- Al is highly reactive
- Should corrode rapidly
- But doesn't!

Why?

Fresh Al surface:
Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃ (thin layer instantly forms)

Al₂O₃ layer properties:
- Very thin (few nanometers)
- Tightly adheres to metal
- Impervious to O₂ and H₂O
- Protects inner metal

This is PASSIVE PROTECTION
Self-healing: If scratched, reforms immediately

This is why:
- Al utensils don't corrode
- Al used in construction
- Al foil doesn't deteriorate
```

**Anodizing - Making it better:**

```
Natural Al₂O₃ layer: ~3 nm thick
Anodized layer: ~25,000 nm (25 μm) thick

Process:
1. Clean Al article = Anode (+)
2. Electrolyte: Dilute H₂SO₄
3. Pass current
4. At anode: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻
5. O₂ reacts with Al → Al₂O₃
6. Much thicker layer forms
7. Can absorb dyes → Colored finish

Applications:
- Window frames
- Utensils
- Decorative items
- Better corrosion resistance
```

---

**Iron vs Aluminum - Why difference:**

```
Iron oxide (rust):
- Porous structure
- Doesn't adhere well
- Flakes off easily
- Exposes fresh metal
- Corrosion continues
- ACTIVE CORROSION

Aluminum oxide:
- Dense structure
- Strong adhesion
- Impermeable
- Protects metal
- PASSIVE PROTECTION

This is why:
- Iron rusts away completely
- Aluminum survives for years
```

### 2.2 Amphoteric Oxides - The Special Ones

**Definition:** Oxides that react with BOTH acids AND bases

**Examples: Al₂O₃, ZnO**

**What NCERT doesn't fully explain:**

```
Most metal oxides: BASIC only
- React with acids
- Form salt + water
- Don't react with bases

Example: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O ✓
         MgO + NaOH → No reaction ✗

Amphoteric oxides: BOTH acidic and basic
- React with acids (behave as base)
- React with bases (behave as acid)
- Dual nature
```

**Aluminum oxide reactions:**

```
Acting as BASE (with acid):
Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O

Breakdown:
- Al₂O₃ accepts H⁺ from acid (base behavior)
- Forms salt + water
- Neutralization pattern

Acting as ACID (with base):
Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O
               (Sodium aluminate)

Breakdown:
- Al₂O₃ donates O²⁻ ions (acid behavior)
- Forms aluminate ion (AlO₂⁻)
- Salt formation
```

**Why amphoteric nature:**

```diff
! Elements at BORDER between metals and non-metals
! Show intermediate properties
! 
! Al and Zn:
! - Not very electropositive (compared to Na, K)
! - Not very electronegative (compared to O, Cl)
! - Can lose or accept electrons depending on situation
! - Hence amphoteric oxides
! 
! This is why:
! Al extracted using NaOH (acts as acid with base)
! Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O (separates from gangue)
```

### 2.3 Reaction with Water - The Complete Picture

**Pattern:**

```
VERY REACTIVE (K, Na, Ca):
React with COLD water
Violent, exothermic
H₂ gas evolved (may catch fire)

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑ + Heat
(So much heat that H₂ catches fire!)

Observations:
- Vigorous fizzing
- Metal darts around on water surface
- Hissing sound
- May catch fire (yellow flame for Na)
```

**Why sodium floats and moves:**

```
Sodium in water:

Step 1: Na + H₂O → Reaction starts
Step 2: H₂ bubbles form on Na surface
Step 3: Na less dense than water → Floats
Step 4: H₂ evolution pushes Na around
Step 5: Reaction exothermic → Heat
Step 6: If enough heat → H₂ ignites

Result: Sodium darts around, sometimes catching fire

This is why Na stored in kerosene:
- Kerosene less dense, Na sinks
- No water contact
- No reaction
- Safe storage
```

---

**Calcium - Less vigorous:**

```
Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂↑

Difference from Na:
- Less vigorous reaction
- Heat evolved but not enough to ignite H₂
- Ca floats (H₂ bubbles stick to it)
- White turbidity (Ca(OH)₂ slightly soluble)

Why less reactive than Na:
- Ca has 2 electrons to lose (Na only 1)
- More energy needed
- Slower reaction
```

---

**Magnesium - Hot water needed:**

```
Mg doesn't react with cold water
Needs HOT water or STEAM

Mg + 2H₂O(hot) → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂↑
Mg + H₂O(steam) → MgO + H₂↑

Why?
- Mg less reactive than Na, Ca
- Needs activation energy
- Heat provides this energy
- Then reaction proceeds
```

---

**Iron, Zinc, Aluminum - Steam only:**

```
3Fe + 4H₂O(steam) → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂
Zn + H₂O(steam) → ZnO + H₂
2Al + 3H₂O(steam) → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂

Observations:
- No reaction with cold or hot water
- Red hot metal + steam → Reaction
- Metal oxide formed (not hydroxide)

Why oxide not hydroxide?
- High temperature
- Hydroxide unstable at that temp
- Decomposes to oxide
```

---

**Unreactive metals (Cu, Ag, Au):**

```
Cu + H₂O → No reaction (even with steam)
Ag + H₂O → No reaction
Au + H₂O → No reaction

Why?
- Very difficult to oxidize
- Strong hold on electrons
- Oxide formation not favorable
- Stable in metallic form

This is why:
- Cu pipes for water supply (won't react)
- Ag, Au vessels (won't corrode)
- Ancient metal objects survive
```

### 2.4 Reaction with Acids - The H₂ Test

**General reaction:**

```
Metal + Acid → Salt + H₂↑

Example:
Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
```

**What's really happening:**

```diff
! Acid provides H⁺ ions
! Metal more reactive than H
! Metal displaces H from acid
! 
! Ionic view:
! Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation)
! 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (reduction)
! 
! Metal oxidized, H⁺ reduced
! This is REDOX reaction
```

---

**The vigorous vs slow reaction:**

**From Activity 3.11:**

```
Reactivity order (with dilute acids):
Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > (H) > Cu

Magnesium:
- Vigorous fizzing
- Temperature rise highest
- Reaction very fast
- Mg very reactive

Aluminum:
- Moderate fizzing
- Some temperature rise
- Oxide layer slows initial reaction
- Once started, rapid

Zinc:
- Steady fizzing
- Mild temperature rise
- Moderate rate

Iron:
- Slow fizzing
- Little temperature rise
- Slow reaction

Copper:
- No fizzing
- No temperature change
- NO REACTION

Why?
Cu less reactive than H
Cannot displace H from acids
```

---

**The nitric acid exception:**

```diff
! HNO₃ is OXIDIZING ACID
! Oxidizes H₂ to H₂O
! Hence no H₂ evolved (except with Mg, Mn in very dilute HNO₃)
! 
! What happens instead:
! 
! With dilute HNO₃:
! 3Cu + 8HNO₃(dil) → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO↑ + 4H₂O
!                     (Nitric oxide - colorless)
! 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ (brown fumes)
! 
! With concentrated HNO₃:
! Cu + 4HNO₃(conc) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O
!                     (Brown fumes directly)
! 
! NEVER test for H₂ with HNO₃!
```

---

**Aqua regia - The gold dissolver:**

```
Aqua regia = HCl + HNO₃ (3:1)

Why "royal water"?
- Can dissolve gold (king of metals)
- Neither acid alone can do this

Chemistry:
HNO₃ + 3HCl → NOCl + Cl₂ + 2H₂O

Products formed:
- NOCl (nitrosyl chloride)
- Cl₂ (chlorine)
- Both very reactive

Au + HNO₃ + 4HCl → HAuCl₄ + NO + 2H₂O
(Gold doesn't react)  (Chloroauric acid)

Actually:
Au → Au³⁺ + 3e⁻ (oxidation by Cl₂ + HNO₃)
Au³⁺ + 4Cl⁻ → AuCl₄⁻ (complex ion forms)

This is why:
- Used to test purity of gold
- Gold refining
- Making gold compounds
- VERY corrosive - handle with extreme care!
```

### 2.5 Activity/Reactivity Series - The Master Key

**The complete series:**

```
K   ]
Na  ]  Most reactive
Ca  ]  - React with cold water
Mg  ]  - Displace H₂ from acids

Al  ]  - React with steam
Zn  ]  - Displace H₂ from acids
Fe  ]  Moderately reactive
Pb  ]

[H] ← Hydrogen (reference point)

Cu  ]
Hg  ]  Least reactive
Ag  ]  - Don't react with dilute acids
Au  ]  - Found in free state
```

---

**How this series was determined:**

**Method 1: Reaction with oxygen**

```
More reactive metals:
- React vigorously
- Catch fire easily
- Hard to reverse (stable oxides)

Less reactive metals:
- React slowly
- Need heating
- Easy to reverse (unstable oxides)

Gives rough order: K, Na > Mg, Al > Zn, Fe > Cu > Ag, Au
```

**Method 2: Reaction with water**

```
K, Na: Cold water (violent)
Ca: Cold water (vigorous)
Mg: Hot water
Al, Zn, Fe: Steam only
Cu, Ag, Au: No reaction

Refines the order
```

**Method 3: Reaction with acids**

```
Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > H > Cu

Vigor of reaction indicates position
```

**Method 4: Displacement reactions (BEST method)**

```
If metal A displaces metal B from its salt:
Then A is more reactive than B

Example:
Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu ✓ (Zn > Cu)
Cu + ZnSO₄ → No reaction  (Cu < Zn)

Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu ✓ (Fe > Cu)
Cu + FeSO₄ → No reaction  (Cu < Fe)

This gives DEFINITIVE order
```

---

**Applications of reactivity series:**

**1. Predicting reactions:**

```
Will Mg displace Zn from ZnSO₄?
Check series: Mg above Zn → YES

Will Ag displace Cu from CuSO₄?
Check series: Ag below Cu → NO

Will Al displace Fe from Fe₂O₃?
Check series: Al above Fe → YES (Thermit reaction)
```

**2. Extraction methods:**

```
Position in series determines extraction method:

Top (K to Al): Electrolytic reduction
- Too reactive for chemical reduction
- Need electricity to extract

Middle (Zn to Pb): Reduction with C/CO
- Moderately reactive
- Carbon can reduce their oxides

Bottom (Cu to Au): Found free or gentle heating
- Least reactive
- Easily extracted
```

**3. Storage conditions:**

```
K, Na: Under kerosene (react with air/water)
Ca, Mg: Dry containers (react with moisture)
Fe, Cu: Normal storage (form protective layer)
Au, Ag: No special storage needed
```

**4. Corrosion resistance:**

```
Top metals: Corrode easily (need protection)
Bottom metals: Corrosion-resistant (need no protection)

This is why gold jewelry doesn't tarnish!
```

## 3. Ionic Bonding - The Electron Transfer Story

### 3.1 Why Atoms React - The Octet Rule

**The fundamental drive:**

```diff
! All atoms want to achieve NOBLE GAS configuration
! Noble gases have 8 electrons in valence shell (He has 2)
! This is the MOST STABLE configuration
! Atoms react to achieve this stability
! 
! Three ways to achieve octet:
! 1. LOSE electrons (metals)
! 2. GAIN electrons (non-metals)
! 3. SHARE electrons (covalent bonding - next chapter)
```

**Why 8 electrons is stable:**

```
Complete outer shell:
- Maximum symmetry
- Minimum energy
- Maximum stability
- No tendency to react

This is why:
- Noble gases don't react (already stable)
- Other elements react (seeking stability)
- Chemical reactions = Quest for stability
```

---

### 3.2 Formation of Ionic Compounds - Step by Step

**Example 1: Sodium Chloride (NaCl)**

**Electronic configurations:**

```
Na: 2, 8, 1
Cl: 2, 8, 7

Na needs to:
- Lose 1 electron (easier than gaining 7)
- Achieve configuration of Ne (2, 8)
- Stable octet in L shell

Cl needs to:
- Gain 1 electron (easier than losing 7)
- Achieve configuration of Ar (2, 8, 8)
- Stable octet in M shell

Perfect match! Na can give what Cl needs
```

**The transfer:**

```
Step 1: Sodium loses electron
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
2,8,1 → 2,8 (now 10 electrons, 11 protons)

Net charge: 11p - 10e = +1
This is SODIUM CATION (Na⁺)

Step 2: Chlorine gains electron
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
2,8,7 + e⁻ → 2,8,8 (now 18 electrons, 17 protons)

Net charge: 17p - 18e = -1
This is CHLORIDE ANION (Cl⁻)

Step 3: Electrostatic attraction
Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are oppositely charged
Attract each other strongly
Form ionic bond

Na⁺Cl⁻ = Sodium chloride
```

---

**The key point NCERT doesn't emphasize enough:**

```diff
! NaCl does NOT exist as separate molecules
! It exists as CRYSTAL LATTICE
! 
! Crystal lattice:
! Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻
! Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺
! Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻
! 
! Each Na⁺ surrounded by 6 Cl⁻
! Each Cl⁻ surrounded by 6 Na⁺
! Continuous 3D network
! Millions of ions
! 
! Formula NaCl represents RATIO, not molecule
! For every 1 Na⁺, there's 1 Cl⁻
```

---

**Example 2: Magnesium Chloride (MgCl₂)**

**Electronic configurations:**

```
Mg: 2, 8, 2
Cl: 2, 8, 7

Mg needs to:
- Lose 2 electrons
- Achieve Ne configuration (2, 8)

ONE Mg atom provides 2 electrons
TWO Cl atoms need 2 electrons (1 each)

So ratio = 1 Mg : 2 Cl
Formula = MgCl₂
```

**The transfer:**

```
Step 1: Mg loses 2 electrons
Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
2,8,2 → 2,8

Charge: 12p - 10e = +2
Magnesium cation: Mg²⁺

Step 2: Each Cl gains 1 electron
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Two chloride anions: 2Cl⁻

Step 3: Attraction
One Mg²⁺ attracts two Cl⁻
Formula unit: Mg²⁺(Cl⁻)₂
Written as: MgCl₂
```

**Why not MgCl?**

```diff
! Mg MUST lose 2 electrons (to achieve octet)
! Can't lose just 1 (would leave Mg⁺ with 2,8,1 - not stable)
! Each Cl can only accept 1 electron
! Need 2 Cl atoms to accept 2 electrons from Mg
! Hence MgCl₂, not MgCl
```

---

**Example 3: Magnesium Oxide (MgO)**

```
Mg: 2, 8, 2 → Loses 2e⁻ → Mg²⁺ (2, 8)
O: 2, 6 → Gains 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (2, 8)

Perfect 1:1 match!

Mg²⁺ + O²⁻ → MgO

Both achieve octet
Strong electrostatic attraction (both doubly charged)
Very stable compound
High melting point (~2850°C)
```

---

### 3.3 Properties of Ionic Compounds - The Atomic Explanation

**Property 1: High melting and boiling points**

**NCERT says:** Strong electrostatic forces

**Complete explanation:**

```
Ionic bond strength depends on:

1. Charge on ions:
   Higher charge → Stronger attraction
   MgO (Mg²⁺, O²⁻) > NaCl (Na⁺, Cl⁻)
   m.p. 2850°C     > m.p. 801°C

2. Size of ions:
   Smaller ions → Closer together → Stronger
   attraction
   LiF (Li⁺ small) > NaCl (Na⁺ bigger)

3. Lattice energy:
   Energy released when lattice forms
   OR Energy needed to break lattice
   
   For NaCl: ~786 kJ/mol
   Huge amount of energy!
```

**Why so high:**

```diff
! To melt ionic compound:
! - Must break ionic bonds
! - Separate oppositely charged ions
! - Overcome electrostatic attraction
! 
! This requires ENORMOUS energy
! Hence high melting/boiling points
! 
! Example:
! NaCl: m.p. 801°C (1074 K)
! MgO: m.p. 2850°C (3123 K)
! Compare: Water m.p. 0°C (covalent)
```

---

**Property 2: Solubility in water**

**Why ionic compounds dissolve in water:**

```
Water is POLAR molecule:
     δ⁻
   O
  / \
H   H
δ⁺  δ⁺

Slight negative charge on O
Slight positive charges on H

When NaCl crystal enters water:

Step 1: Water molecules surround surface ions
        H₂O attracted to Na⁺ (O side toward Na⁺)
        H₂O attracted to Cl⁻ (H side toward Cl⁻)

Step 2: Water molecules pull ions away from lattice
        Hydration energy > Lattice energy
        Ions separate

Step 3: Each ion surrounded by water molecules
        Na⁺(aq) = Na⁺ surrounded by water
        Cl⁻(aq) = Cl⁻ surrounded by water

This is HYDRATION or SOLVATION
```

**Why not soluble in kerosene/petrol:**

```diff
! Kerosene is NON-POLAR
! No partial charges
! Cannot interact with ions
! Cannot provide enough energy to break lattice
! Hence insoluble
! 
! Rule: "Like dissolves like"
! Polar solvents dissolve ionic compounds
! Non-polar solvents don't
```

---

**Property 3: Electrical conductivity**

**In solid state:**

```
Ionic solid: NO CONDUCTION

Why?
- Ions fixed in crystal lattice
- Cannot move
- No charge carriers that can move
- Rigid structure

Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻
Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺  ← All fixed in place
Na⁺ Cl⁻ Na⁺ Cl⁻

Apply voltage → Ions vibrate but don't move
No current flow
```

**In molten state:**

```
Ionic liquid: CONDUCTS WELL

Why?
- Heat breaks lattice
- Ions FREE to move
- Mobile charge carriers present

Heat NaCl → Na⁺ and Cl⁻ can move freely

Apply voltage:
⊝ Cathode          Anode ⊕
   ←  Na⁺  Na⁺  →
   ←  Na⁺  Na⁺  →
   →  Cl⁻  Cl⁻  ←
   →  Cl⁻  Cl⁻  ←

Cations to cathode (-)
Anions to anode (+)
Movement of ions = Current
```

**In aqueous solution:**

```
Even better conduction than molten!

NaCl(s) + H₂O → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

Ions free and mobile
Can move through solution
Conduct electricity well

This is why:
- Salt water conducts
- Distilled water doesn't (no ions)
- We can't use electrical appliances with wet hands
  (skin moisture + dissolved salts = conductor!)
```

---

**Property 4: Hard but brittle**

**Why hard:**

```
Strong electrostatic forces
Ions tightly held
Difficult to deform
Hard surface
```

**Why brittle:**

```
If force applied:

Normal lattice:
⊕ ⊖ ⊕ ⊖ ⊕
⊖ ⊕ ⊖ ⊕ ⊖
⊕ ⊖ ⊕ ⊖ ⊕

Force shifts layers:
⊕ ⊕ ⊖ ⊖ ⊕
  ⊖ ⊕ ⊖ ⊕ ⊖
⊕ ⊖ ⊕ ⊖ ⊕

Now like charges aligned:
⊕⊕ repel, ⊖⊖ repel

Crystal SHATTERS

This is why:
- Can't make wires from NaCl (unlike metals)
- Ionic compounds brittle
- Break into pieces when struck
```

## 4. Occurrence and Extraction of Metals

### 4.1 Minerals vs Ores - The Crucial Distinction

**Mineral:**

```
= Any naturally occurring substance in earth's crust

Examples:
- Bauxite (Al₂O₃.2H₂O)
- Quartz (SiO₂)
- Clay (mixture of minerals)
- All are minerals

May or may NOT be useful
May or may NOT contain extractable metal
```

**Ore:**

```
= Mineral from which metal can be extracted PROFITABLY

Conditions:
1. Must contain metal
2. Metal percentage must be high enough
3. Extraction must be economically viable

Examples:
- Bauxite is ORE of aluminum (profitable)
- Quartz is NOT ore of silicon (costly extraction)
```

**The economics:**

```diff
! ALL ores are minerals
! But NOT all minerals are ores
! 
! Whether mineral is ore depends on:
! - Metal content (concentration)
! - Ease of extraction
! - Market price of metal
! - Extraction costs
! 
! Same mineral can be:
! - Ore in one place (high concentration)
! - Not ore elsewhere (low concentration)
```

---

**Gangue:**

```
= Unwanted impurities in ore (soil, sand, rocks)

Must be removed before extraction

Problem gangue causes:
- Reduces metal content
- Interferes with extraction
- Wastes energy
- Needs removal (beneficiation/enrichment)

Methods to remove:
- Based on physical properties
  * Density difference (gravity separation)
  * Magnetic properties
  * Froth flotation
```

---

### 4.2 Why Metals Found Differently - Reactivity Basis

**Highly reactive metals (K to Al):**

```
NEVER found free in nature

Why?
- React instantly with O₂, H₂O in environment
- Form stable oxides/hydroxides/carbonates
- These compounds very stable
- Very difficult to reverse

Examples:
Na found as: NaCl, NaNO₃, Na₂CO₃ (never as Na)
Ca found as: CaCO₃ (limestone), CaSO₄ (gypsum)
Al found as: Al₂O₃.2H₂O (bauxite), aluminosilicates

Extraction needs:
- Very strong reducing agents
- OR electrolysis
- High energy input
```

---

**Moderately reactive (Zn to Pb):**

```
Found as compounds

Most common:
- Sulfides (MS)
- Oxides (MO)
- Carbonates (MCO₃)

Why these forms?
- O₂ and S abundant on earth
- Metals react with them
- Form stable compounds

Examples:
Zn: ZnS (zinc blende), ZnCO₃ (calamine)
Fe: Fe₂O₃ (hematite), Fe₃O₄ (magnetite)
Pb: PbS (galena), PbCO₃ (cerussite)

Extraction:
- Convert to oxide first (roasting/calcination)
- Reduce with carbon/CO
- Moderate energy needed
```

---

**Low reactivity (Cu to Au):**

```
Found FREE (native/elemental state) or as compounds

Why free state possible?
- Low reactivity
- Don't readily form oxides
- Stable as elements

Gold (Au): Always free (never as compound in nature)
Silver (Ag): Mostly free, some as Ag₂S
Copper (Cu): Free and as Cu₂S (copper glance), CuFeS₂

Extraction:
- If free: Just separate from rock (easy!)
- If compound: Simple heating may work
- Very easy extraction
```

---

### 4.3 Extraction Methods - The Three Categories

**Category 1: Self-reduction (Cu, Hg at bottom)**

```
Metal oxide can be reduced by heating alone

Example: Mercury extraction

Step 1: Roasting of ore
2HgS + 3O₂ → 2HgO + 2SO₂
(Cinnabar)   (Mercuric oxide)

Step 2: Thermal decomposition
2HgO → 2Hg + O₂↑
(Just heating, no reducing agent needed!)

Liquid mercury formed

Why this works:
- HgO unstable
- Decomposes easily on heating
- Hg very unreactive
- Prefers elemental form
```

**Copper:**

```
2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂ (roasting)
2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂ (heating)

No external reducing agent!
Ore reduces itself
```

---

**Category 2: Reduction with Carbon (Zn, Fe, Pb in middle)**

```
Most common method for moderately reactive metals

General steps:

Step 1: Concentration (remove gangue)
- Physical methods
- Enrich ore

Step 2: Convert to oxide
- Roasting (for sulfides)
  2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂
  
- Calcination (for carbonates)
  ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂

Step 3: Reduction
ZnO + C → Zn + CO

Why carbon?
- Cheap (coal/coke)
- Good reducing agent
- Can reduce most metal oxides (except very reactive)
```

---

**Why carbon can't reduce all oxides:**

```diff
! Carbon can only reduce metals BELOW Al in series
! 
! For carbon reduction to work:
! Metal must have LESS affinity for O₂ than C
! 
! C + O₂ → CO₂ (ΔG = negative, favored)
! 
! If metal oxide reduction:
! MO + C → M + CO
! 
! Is more favorable than C oxidation, reaction occurs
! 
! For Al₂O₃ + C → Al + CO:
! Al has STRONGER affinity for O₂ than C
! Reaction WON'T go
! Need stronger reducing agent OR electricity
```

---

**Displacement by more reactive metal:**

```
Alternative to carbon: Use very reactive metal

Example 1: Thermit reaction
Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + Huge heat

Why this works:
- Al more reactive than Fe
- Al displaces Fe
- So exothermic that Fe produced is MOLTEN

Applications:
- Welding railway tracks
- Repairing cracked machine parts
- Joining iron/steel parts
- Called thermit welding

Process:
1. Mix Fe₂O₃ + Al powder in proper ratio
2. Place in container with hole at bottom
3. Ignite (needs high temp to start)
4. Violent reaction, intense heat
5. Molten Fe flows out
6. Fills gap in rails/parts
7. Solidifies, strong weld formed
```

**Example 2: Manganese extraction**

```
3MnO₂ + 4Al → 3Mn + 2Al₂O₃

Al used because:
- Very reactive
- Can displace Mn
- Produces pure Mn
```

---

**Category 3: Electrolytic Reduction (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al at top)**

```
Why electrolysis needed:

These metals:
- Extremely reactive
- Very strong affinity for O₂
- Carbon can't reduce
- Displacement by metal also difficult
- Only way: Electrolysis

Principle:
Electric current provides energy to:
- Break strong metal-oxygen bonds
- Reduce metal ions to metal atoms
```

**Sodium extraction:**

```
Ore: Rock salt (NaCl)

Process: Electrolysis of molten NaCl

NaCl(s) → NaCl(l) (melted at ~800°C)
NaCl(l) → Na⁺(l) + Cl⁻(l) (dissociates)

At CATHODE (−):
Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (reduction, metal deposited)

At ANODE (+):
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (oxidation, chlorine gas released)

Products:
- Sodium metal (at cathode)
- Chlorine gas (at anode)

Both commercially valuable!
```

**Aluminum extraction (Hall-Héroult process):**

```
Ore: Bauxite (Al₂O₃.2H₂O)

Problems:
- Al₂O₃ m.p. = 2050°C (too high!)
- Molten Al₂O₃ too expensive

Solution: Add cryolite (Na₃AlF₆)
- Lowers melting point to ~900°C
- Acts as solvent
- Increases conductivity

Process:
Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite
Electrolysis at ~900-1000°C

At CATHODE (−):
Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (molten aluminum collects at bottom)

At ANODE (+) (carbon):
2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻
O₂ + C → CO₂ (anode gradually consumed!)

Need to replace carbon anodes periodically

Why aluminum expensive:
- Huge electricity needed
- High temperature
- Continuous process
- This is why recycling Al saves energy!
```

---

### 4.4 Refining - Getting Pure Metal

**Why refining needed:**

```
Metals obtained from reduction:
- Contain impurities
- May have unreduced oxide
- May have other metals
- Not pure enough for use

Purity requirements:
- Electronics: 99.99+% pure
- Wires: Very pure (good conductivity)
- Jewelry: Specific purity (carat)

Method: Electrolytic refining (most common)
```

---

**Electrolytic refining (Copper example):**

```
Setup:
Anode (+): Impure copper (crude copper)
Cathode (−): Thin strip of pure copper
Electrolyte: Acidified copper sulfate (CuSO₄ + H₂SO₄)

Process:

At ANODE:
Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (copper dissolves)
Impurities:
- Less reactive (Ag, Au, Pt): Don't dissolve, fall down as anode mud
- More reactive (Zn, Fe, Ni): Dissolve but don't deposit at cathode

At CATHODE:
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (pure copper deposited)

Result:
- Pure copper builds up on cathode
- Impure copper anode dissolves
- Anode mud contains precious metals (Ag, Au)
  → Separated and sold (offsets cost!)
- Dissolved reactive metals remain in solution

Final purity: 99.95 - 99.99% Cu
```

**Why this works:**

```diff
! Selective deposition based on reactivity
! 
! Only Cu²⁺ gets reduced at cathode
! (Specific voltage applied)
! 
! More reactive metals (Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺):
! Need higher voltage to reduce
! Remain in solution
! 
! Less reactive metals (Ag, Au):
! Don't oxidize at anode
! Fall as anode mud
! 
! Pure copper selectively deposited!
```

## 5. Corrosion - The Slow Destroyer

### 5.1 What IS Corrosion - The Complete Chemistry

**Definition:** Slow degradation of metal by reaction with environment

**Common examples:**

```
Iron → Rust (brown, hydrated Fe₂O₃)
Copper → Green patina (basic copper carbonate)
Silver → Black tarnish (Ag₂S)
Aluminum → White oxide (Al₂O₃, protective)
```

---

### 5.2 Rusting of Iron - The Detailed Mechanism

**From Activity 3.14 - Essential conditions:**

```
Test tube A: Water + Air → RUST ✓
Test tube B: Water only (no air) → NO RUST
Test tube C: Air only (dry) → NO RUST

Conclusion:
BOTH water AND air (oxygen) needed for rusting
Remove either → No rusting
```

**The actual chemistry (complex!):**

```
Step 1: Formation of Fe²⁺ (at anodic areas)
Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation)

Step 2: Reduction of O₂ (at cathodic areas)
O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O (in acidic medium)
OR
O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (in neutral/basic medium)

Step 3: Formation of Fe(OH)₂
Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂ (iron(II) hydroxide)

Step 4: Oxidation to Fe(OH)₃
4Fe(OH)₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃ (iron(III) hydroxide)

Step 5: Dehydration to rust
2Fe(OH)₃ → Fe₂O₃.H₂O + 2H₂O
(Rust - hydrated iron(III) oxide)

Overall (simplified):
4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃.xH₂O (rust)
```

---

**Why rusting is a problem:**

```diff
! Rust is porous and flaky
! Doesn't adhere to surface
! Keeps falling off
! Exposes fresh iron underneath
! Corrosion continues until metal completely destroyed
! 
! This is ACTIVE corrosion (destructive)
! 
! Unlike Al₂O₃ (passive corrosion, protective)
```

**Factors that speed up rusting:**

```
1. Moisture/Humidity:
   - More water → Faster rusting
   - Coastal areas worse (sea spray)

2. Electrolytes (salts):
   - NaCl on roads (winter de-icing)
   - Sea water (salt water)
   - Increases conductivity
   - Speeds electron transfer

3. Acid rain:
   - More H⁺ ions
   - Accelerates corrosion
   - Industrial areas worse

4. Scratches/Dents:
   - Expose fresh metal
   - Create anodic areas
   - Corrosion starts

5. Contact with less reactive metal:
   - Forms galvanic cell
   - Iron becomes anode (corrodes faster)
   - Example: Iron nail in copper solution
```

---

### 5.3 Prevention of Corrosion - Multiple Strategies

**Method 1: Barrier Coating**

```
Principle: Prevent O₂ and H₂O from reaching metal

Techniques:

1. Painting:
   - Oil-based paints
   - Forms barrier
   - Used on: Cars, bridges, railings
   - Needs repainting periodically

2. Greasing/Oiling:
   - Thick oil layer
   - Used on: Machine parts, tools
   - Temporary protection
   - Messy

3. Powder coating:
   - Thermosetting polymer powder
   - Heated to form hard layer
   - Durable, decorative
   - Used on: Furniture, appliances

4. Plastic coating:
   - PVC or other polymers
   - Completely waterproof
   - Used on: Wire fencing, racks
```

**Problems with barriers:**

```diff
! If coating scratched/broken:
! - Metal exposed
! - Rusting starts at that point
! - Can spread under coating
! - Not self-healing
```

---

**Method 2: Galvanization**

```
Principle: Coat iron/steel with zinc

Process:
1. Clean iron article thoroughly
2. Dip in molten zinc (450°C)
3. Zinc coats the surface
4. Forms strong bond with iron

Why zinc?
- More reactive than iron
- Forms protective ZnO layer
- Even if coating scratched:
  Zn corrodes preferentially (sacrificial protection)
  Fe protected!
```

**The self-healing property:**

```
Scenario: Galvanized iron sheet scratched

Normal coating (paint):
Scratch → Fe exposed → Fe rusts

Galvanized coating:
Scratch → Both Zn and Fe exposed

But:
Zn + O₂ + H₂O → Zn(OH)₂/ZnO
(Zn oxidizes first - more reactive)

Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn (at Fe surface)
Electrons from Fe oxidation go to Zn
Fe protected electrochemically!

This is CATHODIC PROTECTION
Fe becomes cathode (protected)
Zn becomes anode (sacrifices itself)
```

**Applications:**

```
- Galvanized iron sheets (roofing)
- Nails, screws
- Water pipes
- Chain link fencing
- Electrical towers
- Ship hulls
```

---

**Method 3: Electroplating**

```
Coating with corrosion-resistant metal

Common platings:

1. Chromium plating:
   - Shiny, hard surface
   - Corrosion resistant
   - Used on: Car parts, taps, bathroom fittings
   
2. Nickel plating:
   - Smooth, bright finish
   - Good corrosion resistance
   - Often under chromium

3. Tin plating:
   - Food-safe
   - Used on: Food cans ("tin" cans are steel + tin)
   
4. Gold/Silver plating:
   - Decorative
   - Corrosion resistant
   - Used on: Jewelry, electronics

Process (example: chromium on iron):
Anode: Chromium plate
Cathode: Iron article
Electrolyte: Chromium salt solution

Cr → Cr³⁺ + 3e⁻ (at anode)
Cr³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Cr (deposited on iron cathode)
```

**Why tin for food cans, not zinc:**

```diff
! Zinc is more reactive than iron
! If can damaged:
! Zn corrodes, may contaminate food
! Zn compounds can be toxic
! 
! Tin is less reactive than iron
! If can damaged:
! Fe corrodes (not tin)
! Fe compounds less harmful in small amounts
! Tin itself non-toxic
! 
! Hence tin used for food containers
```

---

**Method 4: Alloying**

```
Mix iron with other metals to improve properties

Stainless steel:
Fe + Cr (12-20%) + Ni (8-10%) + C (small amount)

What chromium does:
- Forms Cr₂O₃ layer on surface
- Very thin, invisible
- Extremely protective
- Self-healing
- Prevents rust

Properties:
- Doesn't rust (even when scratched)
- Strong
- Shiny
- Long-lasting

Uses:
- Cutlery
- Kitchen sinks
- Medical instruments
- Watch cases
- Industrial equipment
```

**Other alloys:**

```
1. Bronze (Cu + Sn):
   - Corrosion resistant
   - Strong
   - Statues, medals, marine equipment

2. Brass (Cu + Zn):
   - Corrosion resistant
   - Musical instruments
   - Decorative items

3. Duralumin (Al + Cu + Mg + Mn):
   - Light and strong
   - Aircraft body
   - Corrosion resistant
```

## 6. Alloys - Improving Metal Properties

### 6.1 What ARE Alloys - The Real Definition

**NCERT definition:** Homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or metal and non-metal

**What this really means:**

```diff
! Alloy is SOLID SOLUTION
! 
! Like sugar dissolved in water (solution)
! But here: Metal dissolved in metal (solid solution)
! 
! At atomic level:
! Atoms of different metals/non-metals mixed uniformly
! Random arrangement in crystal lattice
! NOT separate phases
! Homogeneous throughout
```

---

**How alloys are made:**

```
Process:

Step 1: Melt primary metal
- Heat to high temperature
- Metal becomes liquid

Step 2: Add other element(s)
- Add measured amount
- While stirring
- Dissolve completely

Step 3: Cool slowly
- Allow to solidify
- Atoms arrange in crystal lattice
- Different atoms occupy lattice positions

Step 4: Alloy formed
- Homogeneous solid
- Properties different from components
```

---

### 6.2 Why Alloys - Purpose and Benefits

**Problem with pure metals:**

```
Pure iron:
- Soft
- Weak
- Rusts easily
- Stretches when hot
- Not suitable for construction

Pure gold:
- Very soft (24 carat)
- Bends easily
- Scratches easily
- Can't make durable jewelry

Pure aluminum:
- Soft
- Low strength
- Can't use in aircraft

Solution: Make alloys!
```

---

**Benefits of alloying:**

**1. Increased strength and hardness**

```
Pure iron vs Steel:

Pure iron:
- Fe atoms in regular arrangement
- Layers slide easily
- Soft and weak

Steel (Fe + C):
C atoms smaller than Fe
Fit between Fe atoms
Distort the lattice
Prevent layer sliding
Result: Hard and strong!

Small amount of C (0.05-1.5%) makes huge difference
```

**Why small atoms make metal hard:**

```diff
! Small atoms (like C, N, B) fit in INTERSTITIAL spaces
! (gaps between larger metal atoms)
! 
! They act like "wedges"
! Prevent planes from sliding
! Increase hardness and strength
! 
! This is INTERSTITIAL STRENGTHENING
```

---

**2. Improved corrosion resistance**

```
Stainless steel:
Pure Fe rusts
Fe + Cr → Doesn't rust (Cr₂O₃ protection)

Bronze (Cu + Sn):
More corrosion resistant than pure Cu
Used for marine applications

Brass (Cu + Zn):
Doesn't tarnish like pure Cu
Decorative items maintain shine
```

---

**3. Lower melting point**

```
Most alloys melt at LOWER temperature than pure metals

Solder (Pb + Sn):
Pb melts at 327°C
Sn melts at 232°C
Solder melts at ~183°C (lower than both!)

Why useful:
- Easy to melt
- Used for welding wires
- Won't damage components (low temp)

Amalgams (Metal + Hg):
Always have low m.p. (Hg is liquid!)
Dental amalgam: Can be worked at room temp
```

---

**4. Adjusted electrical properties**

```
Pure copper:
- Excellent conductor
- Too soft for some uses

Brass (Cu + Zn):
- Lower conductivity than Cu
- But harder, more durable
- Used where some conductivity + strength needed

Constantan (Cu + Ni):
- Nearly constant resistance (temperature independent)
- Used in precision resistors, thermocouples
```

---

**5. Better appearance**

```
22-carat gold:
Au + Cu/Ag (small amount)
- More durable than 24-carat
- Can be polished better
- Better for jewelry

Different colored golds:
Yellow gold: Au + Cu + Ag
White gold: Au + Pd/Ni
Rose gold: Au + Cu (higher copper content)
```

---

### 6.3 Important Alloys - Properties and Uses

**Steel (Fe + C)**

```
Composition: 98-99% Fe, 0.05-1.5% C

Properties:
- Very hard and strong
- Can be tempered (heat-treated)
- Relatively cheap
- Most used metal alloy

Types:

1. Mild steel (low C, ~0.15-0.25%):
   - Tough but malleable
   - Used in: Car bodies, wires, nails

2. High carbon steel (0.6-1.5% C):
   - Very hard but brittle
   - Used in: Cutting tools, drill bits, springs

3. Stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni):
   - Corrosion resistant
   - Used in: Cutlery, medical instruments, tanks
```

---

**Brass (Cu + Zn)**

```
Composition: 60-80% Cu, 20-40% Zn

Properties:
- Golden color
- More malleable than copper
- Doesn't rust

Uses:
```

**Why alloying changes properties - The atomic view:**

```
Pure metal:
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫  ← All same atoms
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫  ← Regular arrangement
⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫⚫  ← Layers slide easily

Alloy:
⚫⚫●⚫⚫⚫
⚫●⚫⚫●⚫  ← Different atoms (●)
⚫⚫⚫●⚫⚫  ← Irregular arrangement  
⚫●⚫⚫⚫●  ← Layers can't slide easily

Different sized atoms:
- Distort lattice
- Prevent sliding
- Increase strength

Different bonding:
- Some bonds stronger
- Some weaker
- Overall property change

This is why properties differ!
```

## 7. Common Misconceptions Clarified

### Misconception 1: All metals are strong and hard

```diff
- Wrong: "Metals are always hard"
+ Correct: "Hardness varies greatly"
+ 
+ Sodium: Can cut with knife (very soft)
+ Mercury: Liquid at room temp (not hard at all!)
+ Gold: Very soft (24-carat easily bends)
+ 
+ Iron: Hard
+ Chromium: Very hard
+ 
+ Hardness NOT a defining property of metals
+ Conductivity and lustre are better indicators
```

### Misconception 2: Reactivity and strength are same

```diff
- Wrong: "More reactive = Stronger metal"
+ Correct: "Reactivity and strength are DIFFERENT properties"
+ 
+ Sodium: Very reactive BUT very soft/weak
+ Gold: Very unreactive BUT very soft
+ Iron: Moderately reactive AND moderately strong
+ 
+ Reactivity = Ease of losing electrons (chemical)
+ Strength = Resistance to deformation (physical)
+ Not related!
```

### Misconception 3: All ores are minerals

```diff
- Wrong: "Ore and mineral are the same thing"
+ Correct: "All ores are minerals, but not all minerals are ores"
+ 
+ Mineral = Any natural substance
+ Ore = Mineral from which metal can be extracted PROFITABLY
+ 
+ Quartz (SiO₂) = Mineral (not ore - extraction not profitable)
+ Bauxite (Al₂O₃.2H₂O) = Mineral AND ore (profitable extraction)
```

### Misconception 4: Roasting and calcination are same

```diff
- Wrong: "Both convert ore to oxide, so same thing"
+ Correct: "Different processes for different ores"
+ 
+ Roasting:
+ - For SULFIDE ores
+ - Heat in EXCESS air
+ - Produces SO₂
+ - 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂
+ 
+ Calcination:
+ - For CARBONATE ores
+ - Heat in LIMITED air
+ - Produces CO₂
+ - ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂
+ 
+ Both produce oxide, but process differs!
```

### Misconception 5: Galvanization paints iron with zinc

```diff
- Wrong: "Galvanization is just zinc paint"
+ Correct: "Dipping in molten zinc, forms metal bond"
+ 
+ NOT painting (paint can peel off)
+ Dipping in molten zinc (450°C)
+ Zinc bonds metallically with iron
+ Very strong adhesion
+ Even if scratched, zinc protects (sacrificial)
+ 
+ Zinc paint = Temporary protection
+ Galvanization = Long-term protection
```

### Misconception 6: Alloys are chemical compounds

```diff
- Wrong: "Alloy is chemical combination of metals"
+ Correct: "Alloy is PHYSICAL mixture (solid solution)"
+ 
+ Chemical compound:
+ - Fixed composition
+ - Definite formula
+ - New properties
+ - Example: NaCl
+ 
+ Alloy:
+ - Variable composition
+ - No definite formula
+ - Intermediate properties
+ - Example: Steel (variable C content)
+ 
+ Alloys formed by MELTING + MIXING (physical)
+ Not by chemical reaction
```

### Misconception 7: Amphoteric means reacts with everything

```diff
- Wrong: "Amphoteric oxide reacts with all substances"
+ Correct: "Reacts with BOTH acids AND bases specifically"
+ 
+ Amphoteric = Dual nature
+ Can act as acid OR base depending on what it meets
+ 
+ With acid: Acts as base (accepts H⁺)
+ With base: Acts as acid (donates O²⁻)
+ 
+ Only certain oxides: Al₂O₃, ZnO, PbO, SnO
+ Not all metal oxides!
```

### Misconception 8: More reactive metals easier to extract

```diff
- Wrong: "Reactive metals easier to get from ores"
+ Correct: "OPPOSITE! More reactive = Harder to extract"
+ 
+ Highly reactive (K, Na, Al):
+ - Form very stable compounds
+ - Strong metal-oxygen bonds
+ - Need electrolysis (difficult, expensive)
+ 
+ Less reactive (Cu, Ag, Au):
+ - Form unstable compounds (or found free)
+ - Weak bonds
+ - Simple heating works (easy, cheap)
+ 
+ Reactivity ∝ Extraction difficulty
```

### Misconception 9: Rust is just iron oxide

```diff
- Wrong: "Rust = Fe₂O₃"
+ Correct: "Rust = HYDRATED iron oxide (Fe₂O₃.xH₂O)"
+ 
+ Anhydrous Fe₂O₃:
+ - Red powder
+ - Tightly packed
+ - Somewhat protective
+ 
+ Rust (Fe₂O₃.xH₂O):
+ - Brown, flaky
+ - Porous structure
+ - Contains water molecules
+ - NOT protective
+ - Keeps flaking off
+ 
+ Water essential part of rust structure!
```

### Misconception 10: Ionic bonds are weak because compounds melt

```diff
- Wrong: "Ionic compounds melt, so bonds must be weak"
+ Correct: "Very HIGH melting points show bonds are STRONG"
+ 
+ NaCl melts at 801°C (1074 K)
+ MgO melts at 2850°C (3123 K)
+ Compare: Water melts at 0°C (covalent)
+ 
+ High m.p. = Strong bonds
+ 
+ But:
+ Once molten, ions can move (conduct electricity)
+ In solid, ions fixed (don't conduct)
+ 
+ Melting breaks lattice, not bonds completely
```

### Displacement Reaction Problems

**Quick method:**

```
Question: Will A displace B from its salt?

Step 1: Locate both in reactivity series
Step 2: Compare positions
Step 3: Apply rule

Rule:
If A ABOVE B → YES, displacement occurs
If A BELOW B → NO, no reaction

Example:
Will Fe displace Cu from CuSO₄?

Series: ... Fe ... Cu ...
Fe above Cu → YES

Equation: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu ✓
```

---

### Extraction Method Decision

**Decision tree:**

```
Given: Metal to extract

Step 1: Locate in reactivity series

Is it K to Al (top)?
→ YES: Electrolytic reduction
→ Example: Na by electrolysis of molten NaCl

Is it Zn to Pb (middle)?
→ YES: Reduction with carbon
→ Example: ZnO + C → Zn + CO

Is it Cu to Au (bottom)?
→ YES: Self-reduction or simple heating
→ Example: 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂

Write this pattern in exam if asked!
```

---

### Ionic Compound Formation

**Step-by-step approach:**

```
Question: Show formation of MgO

Step 1: Write electronic configurations
Mg: 2, 8, 2
O: 2, 6

Step 2: Determine electron transfer
Mg needs to lose: 2 electrons (to achieve 2, 8)
O needs to gain: 2 electrons (to achieve 2, 8)

Perfect 1:1 match!

Step 3: Show transfer
Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻

Step 4: Write formula
Mg²⁺ + O²⁻ → MgO

Step 5: Draw electron-dot structure (if asked)
[Show dots for all electrons, cross for transferred]

Always show all steps for full marks!
```

### Properties of Ionic Compounds

**Question pattern:** Why do ionic compounds have high m.p.?

**Answer format:**

```
Must include:

1. Structure:
   "Ionic compounds exist as crystal lattice"
   "Strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions"

2. Energy perspective:
   "Large amount of energy required to break these forces"
   "Overcome attraction between cations and anions"

3. Conclusion:
   "Hence high melting and boiling points"

4. Example (for extra marks):
   "NaCl melts at 801°C"

Write in logical flow, not point form!
```

### Corrosion Prevention - Exam Answers

**Question:** How to prevent rusting?

**Good answer format:**

```
Method 1: Barrier methods
- Painting, oiling, greasing
- Prevents contact with O₂ and H₂O
- Example: Car bodies painted

Method 2: Galvanization
- Coating with zinc
- Zn more reactive, corrodes first
- Sacrificial protection
- Even if scratched, Fe protected

Method 3: Alloying
- Stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni)
- Cr forms protective Cr₂O₃ layer
- Doesn't rust

Explain HOW each method works, not just name it!
```

```
- Metal displaced by all → Least reactive
- Build series from observations

Example:
A displaces B, C
B displaces C only
C displaces none

Order: A > B > C (reactivity)
```

## 11. Key Equations to Remember

### Reactions with Oxygen

```
1. 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O (vigorous)
2. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (bright white flame)
3. 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃ (intense heat and light)
4. 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO (black coating on heating)
```

### Reactions with Water

```
1. 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂ (violent, H₂ may ignite)
2. Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂ (vigorous)
3. Mg + 2H₂O(hot) → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂
4. 2Al + 3H₂O(steam) → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂
5. 3Fe + 4H₂O(steam) → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂
```

### Reactions with Acids

```
1. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ (vigorous)
2. Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂ (steady reaction)
3. Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂ (slow reaction)
4. 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂ (moderate)
```

### Amphoteric Oxide Reactions

```
1. Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O (acts as base)
2. Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O (acts as acid)
3. ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O (acts as base)
4. ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O (acts as acid)
```

### Extraction Reactions

```
Roasting:
1. 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂

Calcination:
2. ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂

Reduction:
3. ZnO + C → Zn + CO
4. Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

Thermit:
5. Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + Heat

Self-reduction:
6. 2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂
```
